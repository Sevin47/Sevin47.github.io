Ember Line

Wildfire containment on the real world, cell by cell.

Play it Source All projects

A fire starts at real coordinates in real terrain. You send engines, hand crews, dozers, helicopters and air tankers to cut line, drop water and defend structures for one 90 minute operational period, while the wind shifts underneath you.

You are graded on structures lost against the briefed par, how much perimeter you held, and what it cost. Acreage is not the score.

The map is the fuel model

The obvious approach is to generate terrain from noise, and the problem with that is you can see the basemap. You end up watching a fire walk across a lake that is right there on screen.

So the game classifies fuel by reading the basemap's pixels. CARTO's Voyager style paints water, vegetation, buildings and roads in tightly separated colors, so drawing a tile to an offscreen canvas and counting pixel classes over a cell's block gives a real land cover read. Lake Tahoe comes back 100% water, Table Mountain 95% timber, central Denver mostly urban. The lake you can see is the lake the fire stops at.

A model with one knob

The first spread model rolled a probability each second for each burning cell to catch its neighbors. On a fixed grid that has a percolation threshold, and the transition across it is nearly a step. On one incident a rate multiplier of 0.33 died at 24 cells while 0.5 burned 1,038 and was still going. How fast it spread and whether it spread at all were the same setting.

The replacement pushes preheat dose into neighboring cells each second, scaled by wind alignment, dryness, moisture, retardant and partial line, and a cell ignites when dose crosses its own threshold. Spread became certain and its speed became what the rate says, so fuel rates could be derived from real published rates of spread and actually hit them. The randomness moved into per-cell ignition thresholds, where it roughens the perimeter without deciding whether a fire lives.

Six identical results

While tuning I swept the rate multiplier across a 6x range from the browser console, and every run came back the same.

A dev server can hand a console-issued import() a second copy of a module, so I was setting the rate on one object while the simulation read another. Nothing errored. The numbers just refused to move. There are now two exports the game itself never calls, which exist so tuning from outside touches the object being read.

Basemap and data credits: CARTO and OpenStreetMap contributors. Incidents are fictional, set on real terrain. They are not reconstructions of any historical fire, and this is not a training tool.
